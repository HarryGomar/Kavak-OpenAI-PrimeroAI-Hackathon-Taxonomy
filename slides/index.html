Different shops, different words: one product each. The tree is what we build.

The task comes from our own work. To understand a market you connect its listings into product trees. Every shop writes the same product differently: Amazon says Double Stuf Golden, Walmart says Golden Double Stuf; Walmart repeats the brand, TikTok writes it in capitals. Under the noise, these six listings are three products: Oreo Double Stuf Golden, Lattafa Khamrah, and Khamrah Qahwa. Until you know that, you can't add up a product's price, reviews or demand. It looks trivial for a person and is hard for an agent: a size or a 12-pack is never a new product, but a flanker like Khamrah Qahwa is.

These are the three things we want to improve. Accuracy: an agent on every listing places 85% of fragrance listings exactly right, but it can't check its own work, as we just saw. Rules plus the agent get 75% today, at a ninth of the cost: closing that gap is what loops 1 and 2 are for. Loop 1 learns rules from a few labels, and loop 2 makes the check stricter. On food, a new domain, the agent gets 45% and rules plus the agent 20%: that's where loop 3 has to help. Cost: the agent uses about 630 tokens a listing. For two million listings that's about 1.3 billion tokens, around $3,800 a pass, and we pay it again on every refresh and in every domain. Rules place 89% of fragrance listings for free, so the bill falls to about $400. Speed: at a second a listing, one call at a time, that's about 570 hours. Rules take a millisecond a listing, and loop 3 lets each new domain start from the last one's lessons.

Loop 1 doesn't touch the agent. It changes the rules, and the rules are three parts, each a file you can read. Memory is what words mean: the vocabulary. Loop 1 learned that hair, travel and mist are never part of a name, and that Elixir is not a concentration. A point and a half. Operators are the steps, in code. The lattice is one: it counts a name across a brand's titles; Khamrah Qahwa, in two titles, is a product; Khamrah, in three and shared, is a family. Loop 1 added rungs to find the brand, from the brand's own store and from the shop's URL, and a step that settles a name seen in three listings or fewer as its own product, which came from loop 2. Fifteen points. Tools are checks that block a wrong placement: a gift set is never one product. Loop 1 kept the set check on; on food, turning it off adds 501 wrong placements. A form check didn't help, so the gate dropped it. How each change is made: a proposer agent reads the errors on gold and proposes one change to one part, and the gate keeps it only if sealed gold rises. Together, exact match went from 52% to 69%.

Two loops: rules ↔ silver, validator ↔ gold.

Two loops, joined by one arrow. Blue, loop 1: the rules, then the placer agent, place every listing, and we check them against silver. Where the rules are wrong, the proposer changes the rules' memory, operators and tools, the parts we just opened up, and round we go, until the rules agree with silver. Silver comes from the validator: that is the arrow in the middle. Orange, loop 2: the validator labels listings and is scored on gold, the few listings a person labelled. Where it labels unlike us, the improver agent edits its prompt and its questions, and the new validator replaces the old one. A better validator writes better silver, so loop 1 runs again against a better check. Round again, until silver agrees with gold.

Gold is the truth. The validator learns to look like it.

v1 and v2: real, fragrance improver round 1, the same 50 held-out labels. vN is the goal, not a result.

The validator grades a big random sample of the rules' placements, say 500. A person can't check 500, so we draw 50 at random and the person labels those: that's gold, the truth. Each big dot is one of those 50: green when the validator agrees with the person, red when it doesn't. The small grey dots are the 450 nobody checked. Validator v1 is red on 12 of the 50. The improver reads the red ones and makes one change; v2 is red on 3. The change moves every grade, the grey ones too, and because the 50 were drawn at random they stand for the rest. Then we loop: a new random 500, a new random 50, one more change, until the 50 come back all green. That last picture is the goal, not a result. One warning from this run: these labels covered three brands, and on 60 other brands v2 did worse, so we dropped it. That's why the 50 have to be random.

Loop 2, validation: improve the eval itself.

Each new eval: loop 1 adds rules and climbs again

Errors it found, like short names seen once, became rule 6 in loop 1.

A small gold set doesn't cover everything, so loop 2 improves the eval itself. Going round the loop: each round, the validator labels a fresh sample of 40 listings, blind. That's silver. A person labels the same 40. That's gold. We compare them: how often silver matches gold tells us how good the validator is. From the differences, the improver writes a better card for the validator, and we go again on a new sample. The card is kept only if it matches the person more: v1 to v2 was kept, and its flags went from 79% right to 96%. Round by round the validator matched the person 75%, 82.5%, 85%, then 57.5% on round 4's sample. The new cards tried in rounds 2 and 4 matched no better, so they were dropped. What the differences show also goes to loop 1: short names seen only once became rule 6. On the graph: each eval gets a full loop 1 climb. Eval 1 is gold alone: loop 1 climbs from 52% to 69%. Loop 2 then builds eval 2: the validator's silver, and the flags a person settled as new gold. On it the solver scores lower, lowest on silver, and loop 1 climbs again. Eval 3 has more of both, labelled by a better validator, version 2, which matched gold 85% of the time against 82.5% for version 1. Each climb adds rules: five on eval 1, eight by eval 2, ten by eval 3. Each time the gap between silver and gold closes, and the loop runs until silver matches gold. We improve two things at once: the eval, and the solver underneath it.

Measured: fragrance's and food's own loop 1, and food's cold start. Other cells illustrative.

Every domain runs on its own branch, and distil, which is code, turns each change the gate keeps into a lesson with its scope and its record. Loop 3 tries each domain's lessons on every other domain's sealed gold. Food's cleaning lessons carry: dropping sizes, multipacks and shop noise, and reading the brand from the shop's own store, makes fragrance titles clean too. Fragrance's structure lessons don't carry back: a flanker like Khamrah Qahwa is a new product, but a new Oreo flavour is a variant. On fragrance's rules food starts at 11% exact, where the agent alone gets 45%. So a lesson goes global only once two domains have kept it. Next: customer-feedback models, and other tasks with no ground truth.

Taxo improves the system and its eval together. With no ground truth, a system only gets as good as the eval it is measured on, so we improve both. Loop 1 makes better rules: an agent proposes one change to memory, operators or tools, and a gate keeps it only if sealed gold rises. Exact match went from 52% to 69% on fragrance, and agent calls fell by almost two thirds. Loop 2 makes a better eval: a validator labels a sample, a person labels it too, and the differences improve the validator. Its flags went from 79% right to 96%. Loop 3 carries lessons: what one domain keeps is tried on the others. Operators, like the lattice, carry as code; memory goes global once two domains keep it. The shared set check alone keeps 501 wrong placements out of food. We think this carries to other tasks with no ground truth, like customer-feedback models.
